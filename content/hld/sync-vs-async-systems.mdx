# Synchronous vs Asynchronous Systems

**When should the caller wait** for a result, and **when** should it fire-and-forget and get an answer later? That choice shapes **latency**, **throughput**, **failure handling**, and **how services talk to each other**.

Narrative sections explain the ideas; **tables** are used where comparing options in rows and columns actually helps.

## The restaurant analogy

<MetricTable
  title="Fine dining vs food court"
  subtitle="Same idea as sync vs async: blocking wait vs acknowledge-then-continue."
  columns={['Style', 'What happens', 'Maps to']}
  rows={[
    ['Fine dining (sync)', 'Waiter takes your order, stays tied to the kitchen until plates are ready, then serves you', 'Caller blocks until the operation completes'],
    ['Food court (async)', 'You order, get a buzzer, sit down; kitchen works while you wait elsewhere; buzzer when ready', 'Caller continues; result arrives later (callback, promise, message, webhook)'],
  ]}
/>

**Definitions:**

- **Synchronous** — the **caller waits** (conceptually blocks) until the callee finishes and returns.
- **Asynchronous** — the **caller gets control back quickly**; completion is signaled later (event loop, queue consumer, notification).

## Visual comparison (workflow)

**Synchronous** — one straight line: send → work (caller blocked) → full response.

**Asynchronous** — fast ack, then **parallel paths**: the caller keeps moving while a **background pipeline** (queue → worker → delivery) finishes the job.

## When to use each

<MetricTable
  title="Rule of thumb"
  subtitle="If the user must have the result to move on, bias sync (or sync-looking UX with fast servers). If the result can arrive later, bias async."
  columns={['Bias synchronous', 'Bias asynchronous']}
  rows={[
    ['Result needed immediately (login, payment auth, search as you type)', 'Long work (video transcoding, big reports)'],
    ['Work is usually fast (e.g. well under your SLO)', 'Fire-and-forget is OK (analytics, emails, fan-out)'],
    ['Strict ordering is required in one thread of execution', 'You need high throughput and decoupled consumers'],
    ['Simple request–response and easier debugging', 'Retries, DLQs, and idempotency are acceptable trade-offs'],
  ]}
/>

<Callout variant="tip" title="Most production systems mix both">
**User-facing paths** are often synchronous *from the user’s perspective* (one HTTP round trip), while **everything behind** the API may be async (queues, events, workers).
</Callout>

## Key takeaways

<SummaryBox title="Sync vs async — recap">
- **Synchronous** keeps the model simple and gives **immediate** feedback, but **ties up** the caller and can **limit throughput**.
- **Asynchronous** improves **responsiveness**, **decoupling**, and **scale-out**, but needs **infrastructure** (brokers, workers) and **rigor** (retries, idempotency, tracing).
- **Real systems combine both**: sync (or fast) **edges** for users; **async internals** for email, analytics, media, and fan-out.
- Choose using **latency expectations**, **throughput**, **failure tolerance**, and **team/ops** maturity — not hype.
- Know the vocabulary: **queues**, **pub/sub**, **event-driven**, **webhooks** — and when each fits.
</SummaryBox>
